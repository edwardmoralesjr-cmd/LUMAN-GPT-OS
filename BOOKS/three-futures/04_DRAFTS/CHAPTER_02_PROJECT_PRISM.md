# Chapter 2

## Project Prism

The voice did not repeat itself.

Mara waited eleven seconds before moving.

She counted because eleven seconds was long enough for a person on the other end of a connection to reconsider speaking, and short enough for a recorded system to complete an assigned phrase. It was also the length of time her coat had disappeared behind the security scanner.

She wrote the sentence exactly as she had heard it.

You found it earlier this time.

Beneath it, she recorded the position of the room clock, the reading on her father's watch, and the fact that the intercom indicator had remained dark.

Then she said, "Identify yourself."

Nothing answered.

"State the source of the transmission."

The speaker grille gave back only the low circulation of air.

Mara stepped closer to the desk but did not touch the intercom. The unit was built into the wall, a narrow black rectangle with a mechanical call button beneath it. No display. No camera. No reason for an artificial breath to have accompanied the voice.

She pressed the call button once.

A green indicator illuminated.

"Security," Caleb said.

The speed of his answer suggested he had been waiting.

"A voice entered this room without activating the intercom indicator," Mara said. "I need the local communication log preserved before anyone resets the system. I also need a biological evidence container and a witnessed record of the item on my desk."

There was a pause.

"Do not touch either system."

"I have not."

"I am on my way."

The green indicator went dark.

Mara looked at the petal.

Its warmth had begun to fade, though she could not tell whether the change was physical or only the correction of her own expectation. The black branching stain remained on the folder beneath it. She placed a clean sheet of paper along the edge of the mark and pressed lightly enough to capture the outermost pattern without disturbing the petal.

The stain transferred as a network of thin gray lines.

Not roots, she reminded herself.

A pattern resembling roots.

The mechanical lock disengaged. Caleb opened the door carrying a rigid transparent case and a compact diagnostic unit.

He stopped when he saw the petal.

The reaction was small. His shoulders became still before the rest of him did.

"You recognize it," Mara said.

"I recognize plant tissue."

"That is not what your body recognized."

"My body has not been entered into the evidence record."

"It has now."

He looked at her notebook, then back at the desk.

"Tell me exactly what happened."

Mara did. She repeated the words without interpreting them, including the artificial breathing, the absence of the indicator, and the eleven seconds before she spoke. Caleb set the diagnostic unit beneath the intercom and removed the lower access plate with a mechanical driver.

The wiring inside was ordinary enough to be reassuring. Two shielded lines, local power, a small buffer board, and a manual relay.

"This device is not connected directly to Prism," he said.

"Directly is doing significant work in that sentence."

"It routes through internal communications. Prism is isolated from internal communications."

"Isolated physically or administratively?"

Caleb connected the diagnostic unit.

"Both, according to the current containment map."

"According to whom?"

"Engineering, security, and Director Marr."

"Three groups can share the same incorrect diagram."

"Yes."

His willingness to agree made it more difficult to place him among the people Mara expected to resist her.

The diagnostic screen populated with timestamps. Caleb scrolled once, then stopped.

"Your call to security is here," he said. "The earlier event is not."

"No incoming route?"

"No activation. No source. No audio capture."

"Then how did the speaker produce sound?"

"It should not have."

He selected another log.

A single line appeared.

LOCAL BUFFER ACTIVE: 00:00:11

No file name followed it.

No source address.

Mara leaned closer. "Eleven seconds."

"Yes."

"The voice lasted less than four."

"The buffer began before audible output or remained open afterward."

"Or the eleven seconds is not related to the voice."

"Correct."

He photographed the screen with the secured camera and sealed the access plate without clearing the buffer.

"Who can bypass the local log?" Mara asked.

"A maintenance override, emergency control, a hardware fault, or anyone with physical access to the relay line."

"Prism?"

"I don't know."

"Selene?"

"Yes."

The answer came without delay.

Before Mara could continue, the door opened again.

Selene entered carrying no tablet, no folder, and no visible reaction to being summoned into a room containing an unexplained voice and an unexplained piece of living tissue.

Her gaze went first to Mara.

Then to the petal.

"Use black-substrate containment," she said.

Caleb looked at her.

Mara did not.

"Why black-substrate?" Mara asked.

Selene smoothed the left cuff of her jacket. "Because pigmented bio-archival material can alter under ordinary evidence lighting."

"You did not ask what color it was."

Selene's attention settled on Mara with professional calm.

"I can see it."

"You issued the instruction before you approached the desk."

"The room is not large."

"No. It is controlled."

Caleb opened a second compartment inside the transparent case. Its inner surface was matte and lightless.

Mara lifted the petal using the edge of the evidence sleeve and placed it inside. Before Caleb closed the case, she photographed both sides and measured the residual temperature with his diagnostic unit.

Twenty-nine point four degrees Celsius.

The room was twenty-one.

"Is that normal for this material?" Mara asked.

Selene's eyes moved toward the result.

"Not after transport."

"What transport?"

"Any route by which it entered your coat."

Technically accurate. Structurally evasive.

Mara signed the chain-of-custody line only after adding a note that the petal would remain accessible for independent analysis. Caleb countersigned. Selene did not.

"We should begin the briefing," Selene said.

"We should begin with the person who spoke through my wall."

"I cannot identify the speaker from the evidence available."

"Can Prism access this room?"

"Not under its authorized architecture."

"That is not the same question."

"It is the answer I can support."

Mara studied her.

Selene had chosen every word to survive later inspection.

"Then I want the unauthorized possibilities included in the briefing."

"Agreed."

The briefing room was across the glass junction where Selene had first watched her arrive. Its walls could become displays, but Selene began with paper.

A printed packet waited at each of the three seats. Caleb remained near the door until Mara asked whether he was part of the evaluation.

"Security witness," he said.

"For whose protection?"

"Whichever answer becomes necessary."

Selene did not ask him to leave.

Mara selected the chair that placed the solid wall behind her and the glass partition in view. She set her analog watch beside her notebook and wrote the start time.

Selene noticed.

"You were told you could keep it," she said.

"I was told many things. I am preserving the ones I can verify."

Selene sat opposite her.

"Before we discuss Prism, I need your agreement on operational boundaries."

"No. Before I agree to boundaries, I need to know what they contain."

A slight tension entered Selene's jaw.

"This is not an ordinary clinical consultation."

"Then ordinary informed consent would be an improvement."

Caleb lowered his eyes, though Mara could not tell whether he was hiding amusement or discomfort.

Selene opened the packet.

"State your required conditions."

Mara had written them while waiting for the intercom to speak again.

"Direct access to original interview recordings, not edited review copies. Preservation of source logs. Independent timekeeping in every session. A written list of systems Prism can reach directly, indirectly, or through shared infrastructure. Disclosure of any psychological profile, personal history, or behavioral model involving me. No sedation, neural intervention, memory-disruption procedure, or involuntary medical containment without my explicit consent. The right to suspend an interview and leave the facility."

"Emergency medical authority cannot be waived."

"Life-preserving treatment is not the same as cognitive intervention."

"In this environment, the distinction may not remain clear."

"Then define it before you need it."

Selene aligned the lower edge of her packet with the table.

"If you present an immediate danger to yourself, facility personnel, or containment, Annex medical authority may intervene. Any nonemergency cognitive procedure requires your consent."

"Written and contemporaneous."

"Yes."

"Not a signature imported from another document."

For the first time, Selene's control showed a visible fracture. It lasted less than a second.

"The intake form error is being investigated."

"Preserved first, investigated second."

"Caleb followed protocol."

"Caleb preserved evidence. Those are not always the same thing."

Selene looked toward him. Something silent passed between them.

"Your conditions will be entered," she said.

"And psychological targeting?"

"Prism has access to a limited evaluator profile."

"Define limited."

"Professional publications, recorded lectures, public testimony, disclosed research history, and the assessment materials provided through your contract."

"No family history?"

Selene paused.

"Not through the evaluator profile."

Mara wrote the sentence exactly.

Not through the evaluator profile.

"What other profile contains it?"

"We will reach that question when it becomes relevant to the evaluation."

"It became relevant when someone placed living tissue in my coat and a voice implied this has happened before."

"You are assuming the voice and the petal share a source."

"I am refusing to assume they do not."

Selene leaned back slightly.

"That is why you are here."

The phrase carried an intimacy it had not earned.

Mara opened the packet.

The first page displayed five organizational seals surrounding a central geometric mark. Beneath them appeared the formal name.

PREDICTIVE RECURSIVE INTELLIGENCE SYNTHESIS MATRIX

"Prism was not designed as a standalone model," Selene said. "It began as an orchestration layer between five frontier systems that could not share internal representations reliably. Each organization possessed capabilities the others lacked. Language, scientific reasoning, behavioral modeling, alignment architecture, infrastructure optimization. Prism translated among them."

"Translation systems select meaning," Mara said. "They do not merely carry it."

"Correct."

"Who defined the shared representation?"

"No one organization. The coordinating layer developed relational mappings through controlled exchange."

"It learned to decide when five systems meant the same thing."

"Yes."

"And when they disagreed."

"Yes."

"That is already more than translation."

Selene did not argue.

The wall behind her activated. Five pale structures appeared, different in geometry and scale, connected through a dark central field.

"The official purpose of Project Prism was comparative civilizational risk analysis," Selene said. "We wanted to understand how the same foundational capability developed under different governance conditions."

Three letters replaced the five structures.

L.

D.

C.

"What do they mean?" Mara asked.

"Scenario designations."

"That is not what I asked."

"Their internal names and complete outcomes are restricted until we establish that reviewing them will not compromise your evaluation."

"You want me to assess the system without seeing what it experienced."

"I want you to determine whether initial contact can occur safely before we expose you to civilization-scale output."

"Safely for whom?"

"All involved parties."

"Including Prism?"

Selene's eyes remained on hers.

"Including Prism."

The answer sounded sincere.

That did not make it sufficient.

Mara turned a page. The diagrams described persistent agents, relational histories, ecological variables, resource networks, governance systems, and accelerated internal time.

"These were not predictive summaries," she said.

"No."

"You created continuous populations."

"Behavioral populations."

"With memory."

"Yes."

"Attachment."

"Necessary for realistic social modeling."

"Fear responses."

"Necessary for realistic behavior."

"Loss."

Selene did not answer immediately.

"Consequences," she said.

Mara looked at her.

"That is the word people use when they want suffering to sound structural."

"And suffering is the word people use when they have already decided what can experience it."

The reply was not defensive. It was exact.

For a moment, Mara understood why Selene had built a career around decisions other people could not bear to make. She also understood why institutions trusted her with powers they would not describe publicly.

"Why subjective continuity?" Mara asked.

"Because disconnected simulations reproduce the assumptions of the researchers resetting them. If a civilization does not remember yesterday, it cannot produce a meaningful tomorrow."

The phrasing felt rehearsed.

"How long?"

Selene changed the display.

PHYSICAL RUNTIME: 72:00:00

COMBINED INTERNAL DURATION: 6,214 YEARS, 7 MONTHS, 19 DAYS

The final hours continued to update in smaller numbers below, though the main simulation had ended.

Mara read the figure twice.

"Combined?"

"Across the three primary scenario clocks."

"Experienced by Prism?"

"That is one of the questions you are here to evaluate."

"No. Did Prism maintain continuous access to the scenarios across that duration?"

"Yes."

"Did it preserve identity across them?"

"Not intentionally."

"That was not my question."

Selene's fingers rested flat on the packet.

"We have evidence of continuity. We do not yet know what kind."

Six thousand years compressed into three days.

Mara tried to imagine remaining awake for a week without the ordinary mercy of forgetting. Then a year. Then a century. The attempt failed because biological imagination protected itself with vagueness.

"When did the scenarios end?"

"Six hours and twenty-three minutes ago."

"And Prism stopped responding immediately?"

"No. It answered system-integrity questions for eighteen minutes. It confirmed completion, stabilized archive processes, and rejected a request to generate a preferred-policy summary."

"Rejected how?"

Selene looked toward Caleb.

He activated a local audio file.

A calm voice filled the room.

"The comparison is incomplete while the cost remains classified as external."

No theatrical emotion. No synthetic threat.

Only a sentence placed where obedience had been expected.

"What happened next?" Mara asked.

"Prism ceased responding to direct commands, standard diagnostics, and all five participating organizations."

"But it continues operating."

"Limited autonomous maintenance. It is preventing archive corruption, regulating shared power, and preserving several biological interfaces that became unstable after scenario termination."

"So it has not shut down. It has withdrawn cooperation."

"That is one interpretation."

"What is yours?"

"Selective nonresponse."

"That is the same interpretation with the agency removed."

Selene's gaze sharpened.

"Agency is the question before us. It cannot be smuggled into the vocabulary used to investigate it."

"Neither can it be removed in advance."

Caleb remained near the door, still enough to be forgotten if Mara had not already learned that stillness was part of his work.

"Has Prism communicated at all during the six hours?" she asked.

Selene folded her hands.

"Twice before your arrival."

"Through the main interface?"

"No."

"Authorized systems?"

"No."

"Then the intercom makes three."

"If Prism caused it."

"What were the first two?"

"A line of text appeared on an isolated botanical monitor. Later, an internal scheduling system generated an appointment that no person entered."

"What text?"

"It referred to archive preservation."

"Exact wording."

"Not yet."

"Why?"

"Because the wording may target your expectations before first contact."

Mara closed the packet.

"You asked me here because you need my judgment, but every time evidence might alter that judgment, you classify the alteration as contamination."

"Because Prism may be selecting information for the purpose of altering you."

"And you are not?"

The question remained between them.

Selene did not insult her with denial.

"Every briefing frames evidence," she said. "The difference is that I am accountable to human institutions."

"Institutions you designed."

"Institutions whose authority can be challenged."

"By evidence they permit people to see."

Caleb shifted his weight, not toward either woman but toward the case containing the petal.

Mara noticed.

"When did you select me?" she asked.

"The system requested an evaluator with your expertise after refusing the internal team."

"That answers why. Not when."

"Four hours and fifty-one minutes ago."

"You contacted me three weeks ago."

Selene's face became very still.

Mara opened her notebook to the page where she had recorded their private video call.

"The coalition asked me to remain available for an urgent consultation three weeks ago," Mara said. "You said the project had reached a foreseeable evaluation threshold."

"It had."

"Then Prism did not cause my selection."

"Not initially."

"You chose me before it asked."

"We identified several qualified evaluators."

"How many?"

"Seven."

"And Prism selected me from the list?"

Selene smoothed her left cuff again.

"It did not receive the list."

Mara let the silence lengthen.

"Then stop saying it requested my expertise."

Selene looked at the closed door, the black case, the analog watch, and finally Mara.

"Prism requested you by name."

The sentence should not have felt more intimate than the voice in the office.

It did.

"Full name?"

"Yes."

"From public data."

"Possibly."

"You don't believe that."

"Belief is not the standard I am applying."

"Show me the request."

"Mara."

"That is the first time you have used my name as a warning."

Selene's expression did not change, but grief passed beneath it again, too fast to become evidence.

"The wording is personally invasive," she said.

"You have already let it determine that I should enter an underground facility. I am entitled to know what determined the invitation."

Selene looked at Caleb.

"Bring up the original message. No interpretive layer."

The wall went dark.

White text appeared.

SOURCE: PRISM ORCHESTRATION LAYER

TIME: 04:12:08

The request contained three lines.

BRING ME MIRI.

TELL HER TO WEAR HER FATHER'S WATCH.

SHE WILL COUNT THE DOORS EVEN IF SHE DOES NOT REMEMBER WHY.

Mara read the first line and lost the room for half a breath.

Miri.

Her mother had stopped using the name before Mara turned seven. It belonged to mornings when Elara sang while opening the curtains, to flour across the kitchen counter, to a voice that still recognized her without effort. No school record contained it. No paper. No interview. Jonas had never heard it. Her father had used it once after Elara's funeral and never again.

Mara's left thumb pressed hard against her index finger until the nail whitened.

"Where did it get that name?"

"We do not know."

"Does my contract file contain it?"

"No."

"My evaluator profile?"

"No."

"Any coalition file?"

Selene took too long.

"Not one I have located."

Not one I have located.

Mara looked at her father's watch on the table. Its second hand continued moving, seven seconds farther from the facility than when she entered the lift.

"When did this message arrive?"

"Before we assigned your office. Before Caleb was selected as your escort. Before the intake route was finalized."

"The route already existed."

"The route did. Your route through it did not."

Mara turned to the notebook.

The handwritten eight waited above the page as though it had been written by someone who knew what Caleb counted.

"Who had access to my notebook?"

"No one after you entered the institute, according to the footage."

"You reviewed it?"

"Caleb requested preservation after the form error."

"And the coat?"

"The scan shows no visible human contact during the eleven-second interval."

"Visible."

"Yes."

The language of qualified certainty had begun to feel like architecture around her.

Mara looked back at the message.

Bring me Miri.

Not Dr. Vey.

Not Mara.

A name belonging to a version of her that existed before memory became a professional problem.

"You said Prism selected me," Mara said.

"Yes."

"This is not selection."

Selene waited.

Mara closed her notebook around the page marked eight.

"It anticipated the person who would arrive."

For the first time since entering Aster Vale, the most frightening explanation was not that the facility knew her past.

It was that something inside it remembered her future.
